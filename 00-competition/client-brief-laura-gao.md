# Client Brief — Laura Gao

*Source: 2026 WGY Gao Case Study (archived in `source-documents/`). Facts below
are from the case; inferences are labelled as such.*

---

## Who she is

Born in Wuhan, raised in Texas. Wharton W'18, **Statistics & Information
Decisions Management** — so she is quantitatively literate and will not be
impressed by hand-waving, but she is not a finance professional. Started a small
business while at Wharton; worked as a **product manager in tech** after
graduating.

In 2020 published *The Wuhan I Know*, a comic responding to pandemic
misinformation and anti-Asian racism; it led to her bestselling graphic memoir
*Messy Roots*. Today: bestselling graphic novelist, illustrator, entrepreneur,
educator, public speaker. Her books are taught in classrooms worldwide.

Her stated philosophy: *"The only person who needs to believe in something is
yourself."* And: she "has always approached creativity with an entrepreneurial
mindset… artistic passion with strategic thinking and a willingness to pursue
unconventional opportunities."

**Risk posture, verbatim from the case:** she "has been willing to take
thoughtful risks throughout her entrepreneurial career," she understands
volatility, and she "wants her investment team to recommend an appropriate
balance between pursuing growth and protecting the capital required for her
goals." Note the asymmetry in that sentence — *pursuing* growth, *protecting*
capital. Growth is the aspiration; protection is the requirement.

## The cash flows, exactly

Year 0 = 2026. **All flows occur at the beginning of the applicable year.**

| Year | # | Flow | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 | 0 | — | Present. Nothing invested yet. |
| **2027** | 1 | **+$300,000** | Initial investment |
| **2028** | 2 | **+$150,000** | From advances, speaking, licensing, ventures |
| 2029–2032 | 3–6 | none | No additions, no withdrawals |
| 2031 | 5 | — | ⚑ She must quote co-sponsors a *range* for her 2033 gift |
| **2033** | 7 | **set aside operating reserve, then −$50,000, then decide facility gift** | Order matters |
| 2034–2042 | 8–16 | **−$50,000 × 9** | One per year, beginning of year |

Total contributions **$450,000**. Total operating commitment **$500,000 nominal**.

Living expenses and short-term needs are covered **outside** the portfolio. Apart
from the two contributions, she neither adds to nor withdraws from the portfolio
before 2033.

## The two goals

### A. The operating commitment — a *hard* liability
Ten annual payments of **$50,000**, beginning of each year **2033 through 2042**.
- ⚠️ **Fixed nominal $50,000. Explicitly not adjusted for inflation.**
- ⚠️ **All ten must be funded by the portfolio "with a high degree of certainty."**
- ⚠️ **We may not rely on co-sponsors, grants, program fees or any outside money
  to meet this requirement.**
- Funding beyond 2042 is out of scope.

At the beginning of 2033, *before* the first payment and *before* any facility
contribution, Laura sets aside the **operating reserve**. We must recommend:
1. its **size**,
2. its **initial asset composition**,
3. **how that composition should change**, if at all, as payments approach and
   are made,
4. our **definition of "a high degree of funding certainty"**,
5. **how we evaluated** that level of certainty,
6. the **assumptions** supporting the recommendation.

That six-part list is effectively a section outline for the Final Report.

### B. The facility contribution — a *soft*, discretionary goal
After the reserve is set aside, how much of the remainder can Laura responsibly
put toward building the residency facility? There is **no predetermined amount**.
She recognises that committing everything "could limit her financial flexibility
as the project develops." Remaining facility cost may come from co-sponsors,
grants, collaborators, program fees or continued business income — but, again,
none of that may be used to justify the operating commitment.

We are **not** asked to size the facility, budget construction, compute the
funding gap, or build a business plan. We are asked how much the *portfolio* can
responsibly provide.

## The co-sponsor problem (this is the interesting one)

In **2031** — two years early, when the 2033 value is still unknown — Laura must
tell prospective co-sponsors what she expects to contribute.

The case states the tension precisely: a meaningful personal contribution signals
viability and commitment and makes co-sponsors more willing to give. But *"if
Laura promises more than she can ultimately contribute, she could damage her
credibility and lose the confidence or participation of co-sponsors."*

So she wants a **credible range**, not a point estimate. We must recommend:
- the dollar range she should communicate,
- **how confident we are** that the 2033 contribution falls in it,
- how favourable and unfavourable markets would affect the amount,
- and the range must **protect the ten-year operating commitment**.

We must also **draft part of her fundraising materials** describing this.

**Why this is the heart of the case:** the loss function is *asymmetric and
reputational*, not financial. Undershooting the promise costs her credibility —
a cost that does not appear in any variance calculation. This converts a
standard "probability of success" exercise into a genuine risk-communication
problem, and it means the right answer is driven by the **left tail**, not the
mean. Any team that answers with a symmetric confidence interval around an
expected value has missed the point.

Personal income taxes, capital-gains taxes, legal/regulatory requirements of

project's total funding gap, a residency business plan, and the size or
composition of any separate contingency fund or endowment.

## Inferences and hooks (ours, not the case's — flagged as such)

These are interpretations we may build on. Each needs to be defended, not asserted.

1. **Her human capital is creative-IP capital, and generative AI is repricing
   it.** Laura's income comes from illustration, publishing, speaking and
   licensing. Documented 2024–26 evidence: a UK Association of Illustrators
   survey of 6,844 professionals found ~32% had lost commissions or had projects
   cancelled because of generative AI, with average losses over £9,000; a Society
   of Authors survey found 26% of illustrators had lost work and 37% reported
   income declines; a study of a Japanese art platform measured a ~30% fall in
   attention to human-drawn work. Her *outside* income — the income that covers
   her living expenses and funds the 2028 contribution — is therefore exposed to
   a specific, identifiable technological risk. Standard portfolio theory
   (Merton, Bodie, and the whole human-capital literature) says the financial
   portfolio should be positioned *against* that exposure. This is the most
   client-specific, most defensible original angle available in this case.
   → See `01-research/differentiation-angles.md`, Angle A.

2. **The liability is nominal; that is a gift.** Because the $50,000 payments are
   contractually fixed and *not* inflation-linked, they can be matched **exactly**
   by nominal Treasury cash flows. There is no inflation risk in the operating
   commitment at all. Most teams will reflexively reach for TIPS and inflation
   hedges here and will be solving a problem the case does not pose. Inflation
   *does* matter — but for the **facility cost** and for Laura's real standard of
   living, not for the reserve.

3. **The residency is in Taiwan; the portfolio will be in dollars.** The case
   quotes the commitment in USD, so at competition level the currency risk sits
   with the *purchasing power* of those dollars in Taiwan (TWD ~31/USD in 2026,
   Taiwanese inflation running ~1.7%, below the central bank's 2% alert level,
   with the CBC actively leaning against TWD strength to protect exporters).
   Worth a paragraph of honest treatment; not worth over-engineering, and
   Taiwanese legal/regulatory matters are out of scope.

4. **She is a storyteller, and the deliverable includes a fundraising document.**
   The co-sponsor communication is being graded partly on persuasion. A client
   who built a career on narrative will judge our language the way we judge her
   numbers. That is a reason to write the range as a *promise she can keep*, in
   her register, not in ours.

5. **Six years of accumulation is short for an equity-dominated plan.** 2027→2033
   is six years. That is inside the window where equity outcomes are genuinely
   bimodal and where sequence risk bites hardest — and the value that matters for
   the co-sponsor promise is fixed on a *single date*, 1 January 2033, with the
   promise made two years before that on 1 January 2031. Point-in-time date risk,
   not average-return risk.

## The arithmetic skeleton

Computed by `03-modeling/src/laura_core_model.py`; full output in
`03-modeling/output/core_model_run.txt`.

**Cost of the ten-payment promise, valued at the start of 2033** (annuity-due):

| Discount rate | Reserve cost | Macaulay duration |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | $500,000 | 4.50 yr |
| 3% | $439,305 | 4.26 yr |
| 4% | $421,767 | 4.18 yr |
| 4.5% | $413,440 | 4.14 yr |
| 5% | $405,391 | 4.10 yr |
| 6% | $390,085 | 4.02 yr |

**Portfolio value at the start of 2033**, $300k compounding 6 years + $150k
compounding 5 years:

| Constant return | 2033 value | Surplus over a 4.5% reserve |
|---|---|---|
| 3% | $532,107 | $118,667 |
| 5% | $593,471 | $180,031 |
| 6% | $626,290 | $212,850 |
| 7% | $660,602 | $247,162 |
| 9% | $733,924 | $320,484 |

**Three observations that should shape everything:**

1. **The operating commitment is expensive relative to the money.** At a 4.5%
   discount rate it absorbs roughly **two-thirds** of a 6%-return portfolio's
   2033 value. The facility gift is the *residual*, and the residual is modest —
   plausibly $120k–$250k, not $400k. Any team quoting Laura a large facility
   number has not costed the promise.

2. **Risk does not help where it matters.** From the Monte Carlo: a growth-tilted
   portfolio (µ 8%, σ 16%) has a higher *median* facility capacity ($241k vs
   $172k for a conservative µ 5%, σ 7% portfolio) but a **worse 5th percentile**
   ($0 vs $41k) and a lower probability of even covering the reserve (91.1% vs
   98.8%). Because the co-sponsor promise is judged in the left tail, added
   equity risk buys upside Laura cannot promise and destroys the floor she can.

3. **The defeasance benchmark — the number that reframes the case.** Using the
   actual US Treasury par curve of **15 September 2026** (1y 4.39%, 5y 4.83%,
   10y 5.00%, 20y 5.40%, 30y 5.36%), a ladder bought at the start of 2027 and
   maturing 2033–2042 would defease the **entire $500,000 promise** for
   **$301,264** — 100.4% of her first contribution. The same ladder on the
   2 January 2026 curve would have cost $325,868. In other words:

   > **At today's yields, Laura's first $300,000 can make her entire ten-year
   > promise risk-free on day one, leaving her second $150,000 — a third of her
   > capital — entirely free to grow for the residency itself.**

   Every unit of risk in this portfolio is therefore *optional*. It is not needed
   to fund the residency's operations; it is only needed to make the *building*
   bigger. That is a strategy statement, a fairness-to-the-client statement, and
   a 50-word elevator pitch waiting to happen — and it is only true because of
   where rates happen to be in 2026.
