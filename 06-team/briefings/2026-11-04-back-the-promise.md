# Back Laura's promises before she makes them

4 November 2026. Decision framework adopted; exact holdings and operating-payment
coverage still need an audit: a check of every payment, date and available
dollar. This is not an instruction to place trades.

## 1. What I decided

Protect the residency's annual payments first, invest only the remainder for
moderate growth, and put cash behind the lower end of Laura's sponsor range
before she announces it.

## 2. Why, in plain words

Laura has two different goals. The case requires ten annual operating payments
of $50,000: 10 × $50,000 = $500,000. The building gift has no required amount.
Losing money meant for a building gift is disappointing; losing money needed
for an annual payment breaks the plan. We give those dollars different jobs.

There is another important date: in 2031 Laura must tell sponsors what she
expects to give in 2033. She should reduce the risk of that promised money
when she announces it, rather than wait until she spends it.

**First, a correction to our earlier work.** Its model put the operating
investments at $319,892 in 2027, when Laura has only $300,000.
$319,892 − $300,000 = $19,892 that she does not yet have. The simulation effectively
borrowed that gap until her 2028 contribution. We therefore withdraw the old
claim that every payment was fully secured on day one. We also cannot call a
bond fund's estimated payout a guaranteed amount.

Instead, start protection with money actually available in 2027. When the
second contribution arrives in 2028, complete and repair that protection
before investing anything for growth. A bond is a loan that specifies interest
and repayment dates; use those payments and cash to match the annual bills.
A fund holds investments on behalf of many owners; even a government-bond
fund has expenses and an uncertain final payout. Its cash flows need checking.

The remaining money is the **Gift Portfolio**, intended for the building.
Our chosen target inside that part is **40% global stocks and 60% short US
government bonds or cash**. Short bonds repay soon. Stocks are ownership shares in companies. Global
means spreading ownership across countries and industries. The operating
money is separate; 40% is not the stock share of the whole account.

Why 40%? Our new model compares different stock shares. It starts in January
2028 with 100 units of Gift money after operating protection has been funded.
Its middle outcome for the maximum building gift was 87.1 units with 40% stocks
and 89.2 with 60%. These are gift ceilings **after leaving money unpromised**,
not the total value of the investments: 100 becoming an 87.1-unit gift does
not mean the account lost 12.9 units. The middle gift ceiling rises by
89.2 − 87.1 = 2.1 units. But the poorer-outcome cutoff falls from 71.2 to 65.6:
71.2 − 65.6 = 5.6 less. Five in every hundred simulated ceilings fall below
each cutoff; it is not the worst possible result. We choose the steadier
tradeoff. These are outcomes under stated assumptions, not predictions or
guaranteed minimums.

**In 2031, recheck the annual-payment funding first.** From the remaining Gift
balance, our policy choice is to put 75% into cash and leave 25% unpromised.
Quote only the cash available after costs as the lower-end building budget.
Assume this cash earns no interest. The unpromised part keeps the 40/60 mix.
The choices of 40% and 75% are ours; Laura did not specify them, and neither
number is a confidence level.

One worked model example makes this concrete. Suppose the observed 2031 Gift
balance is 100 units. The model assumes 0.10% transaction costs on the cash
transfer. The backed amount is 100 × 0.75 × (1 − 0.001) = **74.925 units**;
25 units remain unpromised. This is a model example, not Laura's dollar forecast.

The operating reserve is the money set aside for the annual payments. In 2033,
set it aside before the first payment and the building gift. Suppose the
25 unpromised units have grown to 28. The maximum extra
gift is 28 − 25 = 3 units, so the gift ceiling is 74.925 + 3 = **77.925 units**.
Giving the extra is optional after another funding and flexibility check.
If those investments instead fall to 20, there is no extra gift: the backed
74.925 remains available, but Laura has only 20 unpromised units left.
Those examples are generated by the policy model, not market forecasts.

The upper end of the sponsor range depends on possible market outcomes and
this spending limit. We must calculate the chance that the actual gift falls
inside **both** ends of the range. Having cash behind the bottom does not
make the entire range certain.

One exception matters: if annual-payment funding later falls short, repair it
from unpromised money first. If that is insufficient, reduce the building
budget and tell sponsors. Operations always come first. We cannot promise
the same money twice, and the sponsor floor is conditional on the operating
assumptions remaining satisfied.

## 3. What we gave up

Keeping every Gift dollar safe would give a steadier result and wins the
downside comparison. Investing more in stocks could produce a much larger
gift. We choose moderate growth because Laura wants to pursue a building
contribution, but the short period before the sponsor announcement limits how
much uncertainty we accept. A crash before 2031 reduces what she can announce;
moving to cash afterward cannot undo that loss.

We also reject an AI or arts stock theme. Laura's profession does not tell us
which technology shares would offset her income risks. The feature specific
to her is the sequence of residency payments and sponsor promises.

## 4. What would change my mind

Before the strategy freezes on 6 November: an actual payment-by-payment audit
that shows the reserve cannot be funded with the two contributions; fund costs
or payout risks that reduce the surplus materially; or better modeling that
reverses the growth/downside tradeoff. The framework already says to repair
operating protection before growing or promising more.
