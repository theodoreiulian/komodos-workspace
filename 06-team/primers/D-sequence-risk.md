# Primer D — Sequence risk: why bad years hurt most while money is going out

*For P4. Read this, then explain it to the team in 3 minutes, without notes.
All numbers are an invented example to keep the arithmetic simple.*

## The surprising fact

Two portfolios can have **exactly the same yearly returns, just in a different
order**, and end up with **different amounts of money**, but only if money is
being **taken out** along the way. That is **sequence risk**: the risk that bad
years come at the worst time.

## One worked example

Start with **$150,000**. Take out **$50,000 at the start of each year** for
three years. The market's three yearly returns are **+20%, 0%, −30%**, in some
order.

**Order 1: good year first (+20%, then 0%, then −30%)**

| Year | Start | Take out $50k | Left | Return | End |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $150,000 | −$50,000 | $100,000 | +20% | $120,000 |
| 2 | $120,000 | −$50,000 | $70,000 | 0% | $70,000 |
| 3 | $70,000 | −$50,000 | $20,000 | −30% | **$14,000** |

**Order 2: bad year first (−30%, then 0%, then +20%)**

| Year | Start | Take out $50k | Left | Return | End |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $150,000 | −$50,000 | $100,000 | −30% | $70,000 |
| 2 | $70,000 | −$50,000 | $20,000 | 0% | $20,000 |
| 3 | $20,000 | −$50,000 | **−$30,000** | | **ran out** |

Same three returns. In Order 1 there's $14,000 left over. In Order 2 the money
**runs out** and the third payment can't be made.

## Why that happens

A loss early on hits a **big** pot, and then the payments take out money that
**never gets a chance to recover**. You have to sell after prices have fallen,
which locks in the loss. A crash near the end hits a **small** pot, so it does
less damage.

## Why this matters for Laura

Laura pays out $50,000 a year from 2033 to 2042. If that money sits in the stock
market, a crash in, say, 2034 could break the promise, even if the market
recovers by 2040.

The fix: pay each bill from something that **doesn't depend on the order of
returns**. A bond that matures just before a payment pays a known amount on a
known date (Primers A and C). It doesn't care whether 2034 was a good year or a
bad one.

## Word to know
- **Sequence risk** (full name: sequence-of-returns risk) — the risk that bad
  returns arrive while money is being taken out, doing damage that the same bad
  return later would not.

## Say it in one sentence
> When you're taking money out every year, a crash early on does far more damage
> than the same crash later, so the money for fixed bills shouldn't be in the
> stock market.
