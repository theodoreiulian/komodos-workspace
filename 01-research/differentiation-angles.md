# Differentiation angles — how we could stand apart

*Research, not decisions. Nothing here is chosen. Each angle is stated as a
thesis, the evidence for it, how it would actually show up in the three graded
deliverables, the strongest argument against it, and what we would have to
believe to adopt it.*

*Read `past-winners-analysis.md` first — it establishes the bar. Roughly 2,300
teams submit; 50 reach the semifinals. Competence is not a differentiator.
Competence is the entry fee.*

---

## The problem we are differentiating against

Predict the median submission, then avoid being it. The median 2026–27 report
will almost certainly contain:

- A 60/40-ish portfolio with a glidepath to bonds by 2033
- An S&P 500 ETF described as "broad diversification"
- A Monte Carlo producing a single probability of success in the low 90s
- A generic ESG paragraph because the client is a socially-minded creative
- A symmetric confidence interval quoted to co-sponsors
- Sector analysis of holdings, unconnected to the liability
- Language borrowed from an asset manager's marketing page

None of that is *wrong*. All of it is *interchangeable*. A judge reading their
fortieth report cannot distinguish it from the thirty-nine before.

**Three structural facts create our openings:**

1. Wharton redesigned the case this year into what is, in institutional terms, a
   **pension liability** — a dated, fixed, non-negotiable ten-year payment
   stream. Most teams will still run an asset-only wealth-accumulation playbook.
2. **Rates are at 2007 highs**, which makes the liability cheap to defease and
   makes "safety" nearly free in a way it has not been for twenty years. Teams
   working from templates or from 2021 intuitions will miss this entirely.
3. The client's **own livelihood is being repriced by the technology driving the
   equity market**. That is not colour. It is an asset-allocation input, and it
   is unique to Laura.

## Angle A — Hedge the client, not just the portfolio ⭐ *strongest candidate*

### Thesis
Laura's largest asset is not the $450,000 she is investing. It is her **human
capital** — future earnings from illustration, publishing, speaking and
licensing. That human capital is concentrated in creative IP and is being
directly repriced by generative AI. The financial portfolio should therefore be
constructed **relative to** that exposure: partly as a hedge against the
technology that threatens her income, and partly by refusing to compound the
same risk she already carries.

In one sentence: **Laura is already short AI. Her portfolio should not be.**

### Evidence
*On the exposure:*
- UK Association of Illustrators survey of 6,844 professionals: ~32% had lost
  commissions or had projects cancelled because of generative AI, average losses
  over £9,000.
- Society of Authors (2024): 26% of illustrators had already lost work to AI;
  37% reported income declines.
- Study of a Japanese artwork platform: ~30% fall in attention to human-drawn
  work; ~10% reduction in output.
- Effects sharpest in advertising and commercial commissions — precisely the
  licensing and commercial work that the case names as funding her 2028 contribution.

*On the theory:*
- Lifecycle portfolio theory (Merton; Bodie, Merton & Samuelson; Campbell &
  Viceira): the financial portfolio should be chosen relative to human capital;
  concentrated or equity-like human capital argues for tilting the financial
  portfolio away from that exposure.
- Betermier, Jansson, Parlour & Walden, *Hedging Labor Income Risk* (Journal of
  Financial Economics, 2012): households that move into a high-wage-volatility
  industry cut their risky portfolio share by **up to 35%** — empirical evidence
  that real households do hedge human capital through their portfolios.
- Kogan et al., *Technological Innovation and Labor Income Risk* (MIT Sloan):
  innovation shocks are a priced source of labour income risk.
- Huang, Martinez, Pokojovy & Qian, *Hedging AI Shocks to Human Capital* (SSRN,
  2026) — the question is live in current academic finance, not invented by us.
- Chen, *Hedging the Singularity* (arXiv 2604.16997, Fed Board author, 2026):
  argues part of the AI equity premium *is* a hedging premium — investors buy AI
  stocks to insure against AI displacing their own consumption. ⚠️ Caveat we
  must state if we cite it: the paper discloses that it was itself AI-generated.

*From the judges:*
- Zoe McCormick, 2024 finale judge: *"When analyzing someone's investments, take
  a look at this person holistically."*
- Ladi Ayoola, 2025 client, on what separated the finalists: *"beyond that was
  the human part."*

### How it shows up in the deliverables
- **IPS:** one clean principle — we invest Laura's financial capital in
  deliberate relation to her human capital, because her earnings and her
  portfolio must not fail at the same time. Costs maybe 40 of our 500 words.
- **Trading Notes:** a specific position justified explicitly as a human-capital
  hedge, with the counter-argument named in the risk sentence. This is exactly
  the five-part shape of Wharton's own model note.
- **Final Report:** a full section. Quantify the exposure, show the allocation
  consequence, model the correlation, be honest about what a hedge can and
  cannot do.

### Steelman against it
1. **It is unfalsifiable and possibly just a story.** We cannot measure the
   correlation between Laura's royalties and the Nasdaq. We have no data on her
   income at all. → Then we must present it as a *reasoned prior*, not a measured
   beta, and say so plainly. Overclaiming here would be worse than not using it.
2. **"AI exposure is not AI diversification."** Owning a basket of AI names is
   not owning the AI economy; it is a concentrated bet inside an already
   concentrated index. The broader literature's own conclusion is that the robust
   answer is diversified ownership of productive capital — global equities —
   not a thematic sleeve.
3. **Hedging the wrong leg.** The operating commitment is supposed to be
   *certain*. Hedging belongs in the growth sleeve, if anywhere. Confusing the
   two would undermine Angle B.
4. **It may already be priced.** If Chen is right that the AI premium *is* a
   hedging premium, then Laura is buying insurance at an elevated price — after
   a 40% rise in the cap-weighted forward P/E to ~22.4×.
5. **Cheap version already exists.** Simply holding a global market portfolio
   gives her substantial exposure to whoever captures AI's productivity gains,
   with none of the concentration. The honest form of this angle might be *"own
   broad productive capital, and do not add creative-economy exposure"* rather
   than *"buy AI stocks."*

### What we would have to believe
That the hedging logic can be stated *without* overclaiming precision, and that
it changes at least one real allocation decision. **If it does not change a
decision, it is decoration and we drop it.** That test is non-negotiable.

## Angle B — Engineer certainty instead of estimating it ⭐ *strongest technical candidate*

### Thesis
The case demands the ten payments be funded "with a high degree of certainty."
Almost every team will answer with a probability from a simulation. We could
answer with a **structure**: a Treasury ladder whose cash flows land on the
payment dates, so that the payments do not depend on returns at all.

Then the honest sentence to Laura becomes:

> "The residency's ten years of operating costs are not exposed to markets. What
> markets decide is how large a building you can help fund. We can put a range on
> that — and a floor under it."

### Evidence
- **The defeasance benchmark.** Using the actual US Treasury par curve of
  15 September 2026, a ladder bought at the start of 2027 maturing 2033–2042
  defeases the **entire $500,000 promise for $301,264** — 100.4% of her first
  contribution, leaving her second $150,000 (a third of her capital) free to
  grow. On the 2 January 2026 curve the same ladder cost $325,868. *(Source:
  `03-modeling/src/laura_core_model.py`.)*
- **Risk does not pay where the promise lives.** From our Monte Carlo: moving
  from a conservative to a growth-tilted sleeve raises median facility capacity
  from $172k to $241k, but drops the 5th percentile from $40,877 to **$0** and
  the probability of covering the reserve from 98.8% to 91.1%.
- **Probabilities are fragile.** Pfau's work shows the difference between an 83%
  and a 91% Monte Carlo score can rest on a 0.5% change in assumed returns. A
  structurally matched portfolio has no such sensitivity.
- The whole LDI literature (CFA Institute readings on liability-driven and
  index-based strategies; asset-liability management for financial planners).

### How it shows up
- **IPS:** the governing framework — two portfolios with different jobs and
  therefore different risk levels, plus the glidepath rule. This is the natural
  spine of the 500 words.
- **Trading Notes:** a matched-maturity purchase, with the reinvestment-risk and
  sovereign-credit caveats named honestly.
- **Final Report:** answers all six things the case demands about the reserve —
  size, initial composition, how composition changes, definition of certainty,
  method of evaluation, assumptions — almost mechanically.

### Steelman against it
1. **Over-insurance.** Laura explicitly says she takes thoughtful risks and wants
   growth. Full defeasance in 2027 removes six years of compounding on
   two-thirds of her money and shrinks the gift she actually cares about. → The
   answer is a glidepath with pre-committed de-risking, and to *price* full
   defeasance so she can see what she is buying.
2. **We may not be able to implement it in WInS.** If long-dated Treasuries or
   target-maturity ETFs are not listed, the demonstration weakens. ⚠️ **Verify in
   the practice window.** A duration-matched ETF approximation is a fallback, but
   it is a genuinely weaker claim and we would have to say so.
3. **It is not "creative."** The rubric asks for a creative thesis. → The
   creativity is in the *reframing* — turning "how much risk should Laura take?"
   into "what is Laura buying with the risk she takes?" — not in exotic
   instruments.
4. **It might be too conservative to score well.** Some judges may read it as
   timid. → Which is exactly why Angle B alone is insufficient, and why the
   growth sleeve has to have real conviction in it.

## Angle C — A keepable promise: reputational risk as the objective function

### Thesis
The case's most distinctive feature is buried in the co-sponsor problem: *"If
Laura promises more than she can ultimately contribute, she could damage her
credibility."* That is an **asymmetric, non-financial loss function**. Shortfall
costs her reputation; overshoot costs her almost nothing. Standard practice — a
symmetric confidence interval around an expected value — is therefore the *wrong*
answer, and most teams will give it.

We could instead **make the floor structural**: hold the bottom of the promised
range in assets maturing before 2033, so that "at least $X" is not a percentile
but a fact. Laura could then say *"I will contribute at least $X, and up to $Y if
markets are kind"* and mean the first half literally.

### Evidence
- The case's own framing: a *range*, with a stated confidence level, that must
  also protect the operating commitment.
- Our Monte Carlo's p5 column: the left tail, not the mean, is what a promise
  lives or dies on.
- Roy's safety-first criterion; loss aversion (losses weigh roughly 2× gains).
- The 2031 statement is a **two-year-ahead forecast of a single-date value** —
  point-in-time risk, not average-return risk.

### How it shows up
- **IPS:** one principle — we size promises to what can be kept in poor markets,
  not to what is expected in average ones.
- **Final Report:** the recommended range, the confidence statement, the
  favourable/unfavourable scenarios, and the drafted fundraising language. This
  angle *is* that section, and that section sits inside the "Creativity and
  Presentation" criterion.

### Steelman against it
An over-conservative floor understates Laura's commitment and could cost her
co-sponsors — the exact failure mode in reverse. The case says a meaningful
contribution *signals viability*. Too small a number is also a bad outcome, just a
quieter one. We would need to show we considered both tails.

## Angle D — AI as a tool we operate and document (not as a writer)

### Thesis
Judges have twice singled out quantitative machinery: Eric Balchunas (2024)
called teams' use of algorithms and machine learning "next level"; 2025 coverage
lists sentiment analysis, customised algorithms and "AI innovations." Meanwhile
the industry has moved: reporting in 2026 suggests roughly seven in ten buy-side
firms now run AI in the front office, using LLM agents for transcript analysis,
idea generation, risk reporting and monitoring.

So a team that builds and *documents* a small, real, reproducible analytical
stack — and is scrupulously transparent about it — is both on-rubric and
authentically modern.

### What this could concretely mean
- Our own Python: Monte Carlo, bootstrap, ladder pricing, glidepath simulation,
  sensitivity/tornado analysis. **This is our work, full stop** — code we wrote
  and can explain line by line.
- LLM-assisted *screening* — e.g. classifying companies by revenue exposure to
  generative AI, or extracting AI-related risk-factor language from filings —
  where the output is a **source we verify and cite**, and where a human decision
  follows.
- A documented **"AI use protocol"** in the Final Report: what we used, for what,
  how we verified it, what we rejected. Under Wharton's rules every AI-derived
  input must be cited anyway; doing it as a deliberate methodology section turns
  a compliance obligation into an integrity signal.

### ⚠️ The hard line
Wharton's policy: AI is permitted **for brainstorming and idea generation only**;
*"AI-generated work may not be submitted as your own"*; all AI material must be
cited; substantial completion of a task by AI is treated exactly like substantial
completion by another person — i.e. academic dishonesty. Penn may run detection
tooling. The IPS bans citations entirely, which is a further reason its text must
simply be ours.

**Our line, stated once and never crossed:** a model we wrote is our analysis. An
LLM's summary of a filing is a source, and gets cited. An LLM's draft of our
prose is a violation. There is no grey zone we need.

### Steelman against it
"AI innovations" are becoming table stakes and may already read as a cliché to
judges who have seen three years of them. Sophistication without a decision is
the thing Melissa Ko Hahn warned against. **A model that does not change an
allocation is worse than no model**, because it costs words and credibility.

## Angle E — Naming the concentration nobody names

### Thesis
In 2026, "diversified US equity index fund" is close to a misnomer: the top three
names exceed 20% of the S&P 500, and a handful of firms have generated 65–75% of
index earnings, profits and capex since November 2022. Hyperscaler capex is
running at ~$754bn in 2026 (+83% y/y), heading for ~$905bn in 2027 — roughly 75%
of those firms' cash flow, a ratio last seen in the late 1990s. The cap-weighted
forward P/E has risen ~40% to ~22.4× while equal-weight sits near 17.0×; the
Shiller CAPE has been higher only once, before the dot-com collapse.

Most reports will hold an S&P 500 ETF and call it diversification. Saying plainly
that this is now an **active bet on the AI capital-expenditure cycle**, with
numbers, is both true and rare — and it connects directly to Angle A, because it
is the *same* bet Laura's career is on the other side of.

### Steelman against it
Sounding bearish on the best-performing asset of the decade is a real risk, and
the earnings genuinely exist — this is not 1999 revenue-free speculation. The
disciplined version is *"we are aware of what we own and have sized it
deliberately,"* not *"we are calling a bubble."* Note also that if the AI trade
keeps running during our six-week window, an underweight will look wrong on the
scoreboard — which does not matter for scoring, but will test our nerve.

## Angle F — Our actual vantage point

### Thesis
We are four students at the American Section of the Lycée International de
Saint-Germain-en-Laye: bilingual, in the euro area, trading a US market that
opens at 15:30 our time, with **French, German, Italian and Dutch sovereigns in
our permitted bond universe** alongside Treasuries.

That is an authentic voice *and* real analytical content. Lycée Français de
Chicago placed 2nd in 2025 as "Finance from France"; recent finalists include
Brazil, the UK, India, Czechia and Canada. A genuinely European read on ECB/Fed
divergence, euro-area sovereign spreads, and currency risk in a USD-denominated
liability is something most US teams cannot write — and the "authentic team
voice" requirement is an explicit rubric line.

### Steelman against it
Biography is not analysis. This only counts if it produces a real insight — e.g.
a reasoned *rejection* of euro-denominated bonds for a dollar liability, which is
a better contribution than using them. Used lazily it is padding.

## How these could fit together

They are not mutually exclusive. The natural composition:

- **B is the spine** — the structure that makes the promise safe.
- **A is the thesis** — the client-specific idea that makes the growth sleeve
  *hers* rather than generic.
- **C is the payoff** — it converts the structure into a promise Laura can keep,
  which is the thing she actually asked for.
- **E is the supporting evidence** for why the growth sleeve is built the way it is.
- **D and F are how we work and who we are** — method and voice, not thesis.

Sketch of a sentence that would carry all of it (not a draft, a shape):

> *Laura's ten-year promise should not depend on markets, and at today's yields it
> doesn't have to. We secure it outright, then invest what remains in the
> productive capital that her own industry is being reshaped by — so that the
> residency gets built whether or not the next decade is kind to illustrators.*

That is the kind of sentence a judge repeats back. Whether it survives contact
with the evidence is the next phase of work.

## Open questions before we choose

1. Can we actually build a ladder in WInS? Which maturities, and are there
   zero-coupon or target-maturity instruments? **Check in the practice window.**
2. Does Angle A change a single allocation? Write the counterfactual portfolio
   both ways and compare. If they are identical, drop A.
3. What discount rate do we assume for the 2033 reserve, and how much does the
   answer move? (Currently our model's weakest assumption.)
4. What does the glidepath rule actually say — time-based or funded-ratio-based?
5. Where exactly does the floor of the co-sponsor range come from, and is it
   structural or statistical?
6. How much equity risk is genuinely consistent with "willing to take thoughtful
   risks" — is a low-risk answer *disrespecting* a stated client preference?
7. Are we over-fitting to one insight? What does a good report look like if the
   defeasance framing turns out to be less special than it appears?
