# Does Laura's creative income justify an AI investment tilt?

Research for the lead strategist, 4 November 2026. Evidence and recommendations,
not a logged investment decision. Competition facts come only from the archived
case and repository; external sources below concern investments and labour
markets only. No current market prices, yields or valuation figures are used.

## Answer

**INFERENCE:** Laura's creative business supports avoiding an additional
concentrated bet on the same industry. Public evidence does **not** establish
that buying AI shares would reliably offset a fall in her personal income.
The defensible recommendation is broadly diversified global equity exposure in
the money available for growth, with dollar payment matching and protection of
her co-sponsor commitment driving how much risk she takes. Keep the stipulated
2028 contribution in the main case; do not invent a probability of its failure.

## Evidence

### 1. What the case actually gives us

**FACT — archived case, pages 1–4:** Laura is an author, illustrator,
entrepreneur, educator and speaker, with a prior technology career. She is
willing to take thoughtful risks but requests a balance of growth and protection.
She contributes $300,000 at the beginning of 2027 and $150,000 at the beginning
of 2028. Living expenses and short-term needs are covered outside the portfolio;
there are no further additions or withdrawals before 2033. The portfolio must
fund fixed dollar operating payments; a building gift is discretionary. A
credible contribution range must be communicated in 2031 for 2033.

Source: `00-competition/source-documents/2026_WGY_Gao Case Study-FINAL.pdf`,
read directly with text extraction, 4 November 2026.

**INFERENCE:** Neither her willingness to take entrepreneurial risks nor her
profession gives us a numeric tolerance for a portfolio loss. The case does not
give her royalty history, the share of earnings exposed to illustration
commissions, an AI/earnings correlation, or an expressed ethical exclusion of
technology companies. Do not fill those gaps with a story presented as fact.

### 2. The old creative-industry survey claims have some real support

**FACT — Society of Authors, 11 April 2024:** a January 2024 survey received
nearly 800 responses from its members and other authors. Among illustrator
respondents, 26% reported lost work due to generative AI and 37% reported lower
income from their work. These are subgroup, self-reported UK survey outcomes,
not probabilities for all illustrators or Laura.
[Original survey report](https://societyofauthors.org/2024/04/11/soa-survey-reveals-a-third-of-translators-and-quarter-of-illustrators-losing-work-to-ai/).

**FACT / ACCESS LIMIT — creator-organisations report, January 2026:** the
joint report identifies Association of Illustrators survey data with 6,844
respondents, collected in 2024–25, and reports approximately 32% lost
commissions and average income losses of £9,262. Its endnote points to the
AOI's February 2025 consultation response. That original AOI PDF returned 403
when checked here, so the statistic is supported through the participating
organisations' joint report, not through direct inspection of the questionnaire
or respondent-level data. The report itself acknowledges self-reporting.
[Joint report](https://societyofauthors.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Brave-New-World-Report-FINAL-20-1-26.pdf),
printed pages 8–11 and endnote 9;
[AOI original, access blocked](https://theaoi.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/AOI-Response-_-Copyright-and-AI-Consultation.pdf).

**UNVERIFIED:** the older note's Japanese-platform claim of a 30% fall in
attention and 10% output decline has no original citation in that note and was
not verified in this research. Do not reuse it. No allocation needs that number.

### 3. Evidence against an inevitable collapse in artists' income matters

**FACT — Makridis, published 2 February 2026:** a peer-reviewed study combines
occupational exposure estimates with US employment/wage data and American
Community Survey data from 2017–24. It finds little evidence of short-run
earnings declines associated with exposure through 2023, while employment
results are more mixed. Its scope and early observation period do not rule out
harm to particular illustrators or later displacement. They do rule out treating
universal income collapse as an established premise.
[Journal article and abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10824-026-09575-3).

**FACT — Jiang, Taylor and Agnew, March 2026:** a survey of 378 verified
professional visual artists reports strongly negative workplace experiences,
including stress and reduced job opportunities. It provides relevant evidence
of disruption, but not Laura-specific earnings data or correlations with stock
returns.
[Original research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04537).

**INFERENCE:** the studies examine different populations and outcomes and need
not contradict each other. The proper conclusion is uncertainty and uneven
effects, not “AI cannot hurt artists” or “Laura's earnings will collapse.”

### 4. Human-capital research supports caution, not a ready-made AI trade

**FACT — Betermier et al., 2012:** Swedish household evidence links job changes
that increase wage volatility to lower risky-asset shares. This supports
considering future earnings when choosing financial risk. It does not identify
a technology stock that insures an illustrator's royalties.
[Author-hosted paper, prepublication version dated October 2011](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/walden/haaswebpage/laborincome.pdf).

**FACT — Huang et al., working paper dated 6 April 2026:** equity projection
portfolios hedge shocks to some measured skills; modeled welfare gains are
modest under estimated skill processes and become more valuable when skill
crashes are assumed. The paper is evidence that the mechanism is possible,
not a demonstrated Laura-specific hedge or an off-the-shelf retail AI ETF
solution.
[Original paper record](https://ssrn.com/abstract=6532578).

**FACT / QUALITY LIMIT — Chen, revised 4 August 2026:** *Hedging the Singularity*
is an AI-generated theoretical asset-pricing paper, explicitly labelled as such
in its abstract. Its stylised mechanism is not empirical validation of our
client's income exposure. Do not use it as the cornerstone of an allocation.
[Original record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16997).

### 5. Broad global ownership is a defensible default; thematic novelty is not

**FACT — investment mechanics, checked 4 November 2026:** SEC investor education
explains that diversification should occur both across asset types and within
them. A narrow sector fund does not necessarily diversify merely because it
holds multiple companies. International holdings spread company/country risks
but bring additional currency, information and other risks.
[Asset allocation and diversification](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation);
[International investing](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/international-investing).

**FACT — Vanguard, 4 January 2026:** its portfolio framework starts with the
client's objectives and constraints, then the mix of stocks, bonds and cash,
then diversification within those groups; global market-cap ownership is a
reasonable starting point rather than a unique optimum.
[Issuer framework](https://www.vanguard.co.uk/professional/vanguard-365/investment-knowledge/principles-of-investing/the-power-of-diversification).

**FACT — Ben-David et al., revised January 2022:** historical research finds
substantial risk-adjusted underperformance after launch for specialised ETFs,
associated with attention-driven overvaluation. This does not forecast every
AI fund or imply that innovative companies cannot earn strong returns; it
weakens the idea that a compelling theme alone justifies a purchase.
[Original NBER paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28369.pdf).

**INFERENCE:** global ownership lets Laura participate in productive businesses
without requiring us to know who captures AI's benefits. It still contains AI
leaders and can remain concentrated in large US companies; inspect the actual
holdings. Do not describe global diversification as a guaranteed hedge against
creative-income loss or a stock-market crash.

### 6. Match the currency of the promise, not the geography of the story

**FACT — Treasury mechanics, checked 4 November 2026:** ordinary Treasury bonds
pay fixed interest and return principal at maturity; the payment schedule is
different from their fluctuating resale prices.
[TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bonds/).

**INFERENCE from those mechanics and the case:** the required payments are fixed
in US dollars. Dollar assets with appropriately timed cash flows remove the
need to convert a foreign-currency investment at an unknown future exchange
rate to meet them. Being in Taiwan does not turn the stated commitment into a
Taiwan-dollar liability. Taiwan equities or euro government bonds would not
automatically hedge it. Geography is relevant to the building's purchasing
power and disclosed uncertainty, not a reason to change the case's currency.
Fixed-dollar matching protects nominal dollars, not what those dollars can buy.

## Against / uncertainty

- **INFERENCE:** a successful AI hedge is possible in principle; broad global
  stocks already contain large technology exposures, and some creative
  businesses may gain from AI. Firm exclusion of all AI companies would also
  exceed the evidence and impose an unrequested ethical preference.
- **INFERENCE:** Laura may value mission-aligned investments. The case does not
  establish a screening mandate. Ask what a proposed screen changes, costs and
  protects before adopting it; “she is an artist” does not itself justify an art
  or media fund.
- **INFERENCE:** a missed 2028 contribution is a possible real-world planning
  question, but not an established case event. If used as an additional stress
  test, label it an assumption, retain the stipulated contribution in the main
  case and do not pretend to know its likelihood.

## Decision implication

**Recommendation for Q4/Q7:** reject a standalone AI or creative-industry
allocation and the statement “Laura is already short AI.” Select broad global
growth investments on costs, breadth and fit with the spending horizon; choose
the stock/bond mix through the lead strategist's downside analysis. Do not
claim an empirically measured hedge that we cannot show.

**Recommendation for Q5/Q6:** the strongest customization is the timing and
credibility of her promises: keep operating-payment assets separate, reduce the
risk attached to a building contribution before it is communicated, and label
the secure component separately from market-dependent upside. This follows
literal client facts rather than speculative psychology. The lead strategist
must decide the calibration and verify actual cash flows.
